# Prior art: guaranteed preemption via injected yield points / instruction counting, and security of deserialized continuation state

Scope: assessing novelty of whitepaper contributions (3) yield-point placement for guaranteed preemption (back-edge yield checks + instruction-count budget decremented at yield points + checks around external calls) and (4) validation of deserialized continuation state (method identity, yield-point bounds, exact slot counts, slot type checks, type whitelists, depth limits). Origin: Second Life Mono scripting, 2007–2008.

## Q1. Yield point / safepoint placement in VMs (back-edges, method entry)

### Takeaway
Putting polls at loop back-edges and method prologues/epilogues, so that a thread reaches a yield point in bounded time, was standard VM practice well before 2007. Feeley (1993), Agesen (1998) and the Jalapeño/Jikes RVM (2000) all do it, and Lin et al. (ISMM 2015) describe it as the typical design. Back-edge placement on its own is not novel.

### Cited Findings
- Marc Feeley, "Polling Efficiently on Stock Hardware," FPCA '93 (Copenhagen, June 9–11 1993), ACM, DOI 10.1145/165180.165205. It is the classic paper on compiler-inserted polling for interrupts. Page numbers conflict: search snippets give 179–187 and 179–190. — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/165180.165205); [PDF, U. Montréal](http://www.iro.umontreal.ca/~feeley/papers/FeeleyFPCA93.pdf); [DBLP FPCA 93](https://dblp.org/db/conf/fpca/fpca93.html)
- Ole Agesen, "GC Points in a Threaded Environment," Sun Microsystems Laboratories Technical Report, 1998 (Dec 1998). It compares polling (global store/guard page) with code patching for GC safepoints on SPARC. — [ACM DL 10.5555/974974](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.5555/974974); [PDF](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/comp/150FP/archive/ole-agesen/gc-points.pdf); how Lin et al. 2015 describe it: [PDF](https://www.steveblackburn.org/pubs/papers/yieldpoint-ismm-2015.pdf)
- B. Alpern, C. R. Attanasio, J. J. Barton, M. G. Burke, P. Cheng, J.-D. Choi, A. Cocchi, S. J. Fink, D. Grove, M. Hind, S. F. Hummel, D. Lieber, et al., "The Jalapeño virtual machine," IBM Systems Journal 39(1):211–238, 2000, DOI 10.1147/sj.391.0211. In Jikes RVM (Jalapeño's successor), yieldpoints go in method prologues and on loop back-edges. — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1147/sj.391.0211); placement detail from a search-result snippet summarizing Jikes RVM (not read in the paper itself)
- Yi Lin, Kunshan Wang, Stephen M. Blackburn, Antony L. Hosking, Michael Norrish, "Stop and Go: Understanding Yieldpoint Behavior," ISMM 2015, pp. 70–80; SIGPLAN Notices DOI 10.1145/2887746.2754187 (verified). The proceedings DOI 10.1145/2754169.2754187 is inferred and not verified. Verified quote from the PDF: "To avoid unbounded waits, yieldpoints typically occur on loop backedges and on method prologs or epilogs of the application, either in the interpreter or in code placed there by the compiler." The paper also reports that adding a conditional yieldpoint on every loop back-edge grew machine code by 7.2% (13.6 MB to 14.6 MB). — [PDF](https://www.steveblackburn.org/pubs/papers/yieldpoint-ismm-2015.pdf); [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2887746.2754187); [researchr](https://conf.researchr.org/event/ismm-2015/ismm-2015-papers-stop-and-go-understanding-yieldpoint-behavior)
- Lin et al. say that, to their knowledge, "no prior work has conducted a detailed study of yieldpoint design and implementation." They cite Agesen [1] as focusing only on GC-safepoint mechanisms. — [PDF](https://www.steveblackburn.org/pubs/papers/yieldpoint-ismm-2015.pdf)
- C. Click, G. Tene, M. Wolf, "The Pauseless GC Algorithm," VEE 2005, DOI 10.1145/1064979.1064988. It separates GC-safepoints from "checkpoints." Citation taken from the Lin et al. reference list. — [Lin et al. PDF](https://www.steveblackburn.org/pubs/papers/yieldpoint-ismm-2015.pdf)
- Second Life's own implementation: "Backwards jump is one of the places to insert a yield." Injected code stores the PC, locals and operand stack into a frame object, which is serialized with the heap and the event queue. This is a 2015 retrospective blog by Dave Hillier. — [Dave's Blog, 2015](https://davehillier.net/2015/03/11/second-life-mono-internals/)
- Contemporaneous Linden Lab material: the microthread injector (built with RAIL on Reflection/Reflection.Emit) "searches through the script assembly finding points where the script should yield and inject extra opcodes that copy the stack into a heap object." Mono reached the main grid in server 1.24 on 29 Aug 2008. — [Second Life Wiki: Mono](https://wiki.secondlife.com/wiki/Mono); [Mono/2008-01-30](https://wiki.secondlife.com/wiki/Mono/2008-01-30)

### Inferences
- The difference between the whitepaper and VM safepoints is purpose, not placement. VM safepoints poll a flag and park the native thread. The whitepaper's yield points unwind and capture the stack into serializable heap frames. For placement, the whitepaper should cite Feeley/Agesen/Jikes as the precedent and not claim back-edge placement as new.
- Checking "around external calls" matches the standard practice of yielding at managed/native transitions. Lin et al. mention yieldpoints "at transitions between man[aged and native code]" (text truncated in extraction).

### Gaps
- I did not read Alpern et al. 2000 directly to confirm the prologue/back-edge wording. The placement claim comes from a secondary snippet and from Lin et al.
- The exact page range for Feeley FPCA '93 needs checking against DBLP/ACM (sources say 179–187 or 179–190).

## Q2. Instruction counting / fuel metering for untrusted code (incl. bytecode-instrumentation CPU accounting)

### Takeaway
The closest prior art is Binder and Hulaas's J-RAF2 (2004–2008). It rewrites Java bytecode so that each thread counts its own executed bytecodes and polls a consumption threshold at method entry/exit, at loop heads, in exception handlers and every MAXPATH instructions. It can then terminate or throttle the thread. That is a portable, instrumentation-based instruction budget with polling, in the same design space as contribution (3). The idea also shows up in Lua count hooks, EVM gas (2014), Wasm metering injectors, Wasmtime fuel/epochs (2021–22) and Stopify (2018). The whitepaper's likely distinct element is combining the budget check with capturing a serializable continuation, rather than a flag poll or termination.

### Cited Findings
- **J-RAF2 (Binder, Hulaas).** Jarle Hulaas and Walter Binder, "Program transformations for light-weight CPU accounting and control in the Java virtual machine: A systematic review," *Higher-Order and Symbolic Computation* 21:119–146, 2008, DOI 10.1007/s10990-008-9026-4. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10990-008-9026-4); [author PDF](https://hulaas.com/jraf2/publications/HOSC08.pdf)
  - The following is verified from the author PDF (the text was extracted with spaces lost, so it is paraphrased with near-quotes). Each non-native, non-abstract method is rewritten as follows. A call to `getCurrentAccount()` is inserted at method start. The conditional `if (cpu.consumption >= 0) cpu.triggerConsume();` is inserted at: (a) the start of the method and before return/throw, which the paper calls "a form of call/return polling as described by Feeley"; (b) JVM subroutine entry/exit; (c) the start of each exception handler; (d) "the beginning of each loop"; (e) every path after MAXPATH bytecodes. The counter runs from −granularity up to 0. Accounting is per "accounting block" (like a basic block, but method invocations do not end a block). Inside `consume`, "the thread may terminate the component if there is a hard limit on the total number of bytecode instructions it may execute, or it may delay itself (i.e., put itself to sleep)." — [HOSC08 PDF](https://hulaas.com/jraf2/publications/HOSC08.pdf)
  - J-RAF2 uses "the number of executed JVM bytecode instructions" as a platform-independent CPU metric. It rewrites classes before loading and is pure Java. — [Binder, "Using Bytecode Instruction Counting as Portable CPU Consumption Metric," ENTCS 153 (PDF)](https://www.inf.usi.ch/faculty/binder/documents/entcs153.pdf); related: [Java Bytecode Transformations for Efficient, Portable CPU Accounting, ENTCS (ScienceDirect)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1571066105051431); ["Self-accounting as Principle for Portable CPU Control in Java" (PDF)](https://www.inf.usi.ch/faculty/binder/documents/nod04.pdf); ["Enhancing Java Grid Computing Security with Resource Control" (PDF)](https://www.inf.usi.ch/faculty/binder/documents/gsem04.pdf)
  - HOSC08 cites earlier Java resource accounting, including G. Czajkowski and T. von Eicken, "JRes: A resource accounting interface for Java," OOPSLA '98 (SIGPLAN Notices 33(10)), and Czajkowski and Daynès, "Multitasking without compromise," OOPSLA '01, pp. 125–138. JRes depends on native code. — [HOSC08 PDF](https://hulaas.com/jraf2/publications/HOSC08.pdf)
- **Lua count hook.** `debug.sethook(hook, mask, count)`: with a nonzero count, "the hook is called after every count instructions" (VM opcodes). The hook has been in the Lua 5.x reference manuals since at least 5.0/5.1 (2003/2006); I confirmed it in 5.1 documentation mirrors. — [Lua 5.1 manual mirror: debug.sethook](https://pgl.yoyo.org/luai/i/debug.sethook); [lua_sethook](https://pgl.yoyo.org/luai/i/lua_sethook)
- **Ethereum EVM gas.** Gavin Wood, "Ethereum: A Secure Decentralised Generalised Transaction Ledger" (Yellow Paper, 2014 onward). Execution depletes gas, gas cannot go below zero, "thus execution may exit before the code has come to a natural halting state"; an out-of-gas exception reverts state. — [Yellow Paper PDF](https://ethereum.github.io/yellowpaper/paper.pdf); [source](https://github.com/ethereum/yellowpaper/blob/master/Paper.tex)
- **Wasm metering by injection.** The ewasm `wasm-metering` tool and Parity `wasm-instrument` compute the cost of each basic block and insert a gas charge at the start of the block. Calling a host function per block is costly; a later variant keeps a mutable global allowance. DFINITY/Internet Computer instruments canisters the same way "to ensure that canister execution terminates." — [ewasm/wasm-metering](https://github.com/ewasm/wasm-metering); [paritytech/wasm-instrument](https://github.com/paritytech/wasm-instrument); [OpenEthereum Wasm gas metering](https://openethereum.github.io/WebAssembly-GasMetering); [Internet Computer blog](https://docs.internetcomputer.org/blog/features/new-webassembly-instumentation)
- **Wasmtime fuel and epochs.** Fuel is a deterministic per-store counter that traps (or yields async) when exhausted. Epoch interruption checks a global counter that the host increments; it is cheaper but non-deterministic ("based on wall-time rather than an exact count"). Use fuel "when deterministic yielding or trapping is needed." Epoch interruption was added in PR #3699 (cfallin, early 2022) "for cooperative async timeslicing." — [Wasmtime Config docs](https://docs.wasmtime.dev/api/wasmtime/struct.Config.html); [Interrupting Execution example](https://docs.wasmtime.dev/examples-interrupting-wasm.html); [PR #3699](https://github.com/bytecodealliance/wasmtime/pull/3699); [Slacked fuel metering issue #4109](https://github.com/bytecodealliance/wasmtime/issues/4109)
- **eBPF.** Before Linux 5.3 the verifier rejected back-edges outright. Bounded loops came in 5.3, verified by unrolling up to the complexity limit (the program-instruction limit rose from 4,096 to 1M in 5.2). Termination is proven statically; there is no runtime preemption. — [LWN: Bounded loops in BPF for the 5.3 kernel](https://lwn.net/Articles/794934/); [eBPF Docs: Loops](https://docs.ebpf.io/linux/concepts/loops/)
- **Stopify.** Samuel Baxter, Rachit Nigam, Joe Gibbs Politz, Shriram Krishnamurthi, Arjun Guha, "Putting in All the Stops: Execution Control for JavaScript," PLDI 2018 (arXiv 1802.02974). It is a JS-to-JS compiler that provides first-class continuations to support stopping, stepping, breakpoints and long-running computations, and it uses "a sampling-based technique to estimate program speed." — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.02974); [PLDI page](https://pldi18.sigplan.org/details/pldi-2018-papers/25/Putting-in-All-the-Stops-Execution-Control-for-JavaScript); [PDF](https://people.csail.mit.edu/rachit/files/pubs/stopify-pldi18.pdf)

### Inferences
- J-RAF2 anticipates "instruction-count budget decremented at poll points, placed at loop heads and method entry" by 3–4 years, on the JVM and aimed at untrusted/mobile code. The whitepaper should cite Binder/Hulaas directly and position its contribution as combining (i) back-edge polls, (ii) a count budget, and (iii) serializable-continuation capture on budget exhaustion, so a preempted script can be persisted or migrated, not just throttled or killed. J-RAF2 can only sleep or terminate a thread.
- Wasmtime's fuel-vs-epoch split is the modern version of the determinism trade-off. A count-based budget like Second Life's gives deterministic preemption points (useful for reproducible scheduling); wall-clock budgets do not.
- Stopify (2018) is the closest later analog: compile-time instrumentation that yields to an event loop by capturing continuations. It postdates Second Life, so it is later art, not prior art.

### Gaps
- Not verified in the Stopify paper: whether yield checks go at function entry and loop heads, and the exact estimator design (countdown vs. sampled-time "approximate" estimator). I only confirmed the abstract.
- I found nothing primary on how LSL2 (pre-Mono) schedules scripts or limits time slices. I also have no primary source for the claim that Second Life Mono decrements an instruction budget at yield points. The 2015 blog does not mention instruction counting.
- I could not confirm the first Lua version with the count hook; I verified 5.1.
- Google NaCl and JavaScript engine watchdogs were not researched; NaCl relies on OS threads and does not meter.

## Q3. Security of deserializing continuations / execution state

### Takeaway
It is well established that deserializing untrusted object graphs is dangerous (gadget chains, 2015; BinaryFormatter "never safe"; Java serialization filters). Recent continuation systems either say "only resume continuations you persist yourself" (GraalVM Espresso, whose frames an attacker has shown can be forged for RCE, 2025) or sign the state (Racket HMAC stuffer). Validating mobile agent *state* on arrival was proposed as early as 1996 (Farmer, Guttman and Swarup's "state appraisal"). A structural, verifier-style check of deserialized frames (method identity, resume-point bounds, slot counts and types, depth limits) appears less documented as a published technique. It is best presented as applying bytecode-verifier-style checks to continuation frames, with prior art in state appraisal and allow-list binders.

### Cited Findings
- **GraalVM Espresso continuations.** The official docs warn: "Deserializing a continuation supplied by an attacker will allow a complete takeover of the JVM. Only resume continuations you persist yourself!" (as quoted in search results). The serialization page says everything reachable from the stack must be `Serializable` and "(ideally) be whitelisted by a serialization filter." — [Espresso Continuation API](https://www.graalvm.org/latest/reference-manual/espresso/continuations/); [Serialization of Continuations](https://www.graalvm.org/jdk25/reference-manual/espresso/continuations/serialization/); [graal repo doc](https://github.com/oracle/graal/blob/master/espresso/docs/serialization.md)
- **Concrete attack on Espresso continuations** (X1r0z blog, Aug 23 2025). A forged `FrameRecord` chain with an attacker-chosen `method`, `bci`, `pointers`/`primitives` (locals) and `next` resumes into JDK gadgets (`sun.print.UnixPrintJob` `printExecCmd()` at bci 367, then `PrinterSpooler.run()` at bci 98, then `Runtime.exec`) for command execution. The author says Espresso does minimal validation on resume. This is a non-peer-reviewed blog. — [X1r0z Blog](https://exp10it.io/2025/08/hacking-graalvm-espresso-abusing-continuation-api-to-make-rop-like-attack/)
- **Java deserialization gadget chains.** Chris Frohoff and Gabriel Lawrence, "Marshalling Pickles: how deserializing objects will ruin your day," OWASP AppSec California 2015 (Jan 2015). Released ysoserial with chains for Commons Collections, Spring and Groovy. The talk covers Python, Ruby and Java. — [slides](https://frohoff.github.io/appseccali-marshalling-pickles/); [Speaker Deck](https://speakerdeck.com/frohoff/appseccali-2015-marshalling-pickles-how-deserializing-objects-can-ruin-your-day); [ysoserial](https://github.com/frohoff/ysoserial); [video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KSA7vUkXGSg)
- **.NET BinaryFormatter.** Microsoft: "The BinaryFormatter.Deserialize method is never safe when used with untrusted input." DoS "cannot be mitigated with a SerializationBinder or any other BinaryFormatter configuration switch." RCE and information disclosure are also possible. Recommended alternatives: XmlSerializer, DataContractSerializer, System.Text.Json. Analyzer rules CA2300–CA2302 flag use without a `Binder` (a type allow-list). — [BinaryFormatter security guide](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dotnet/standard/serialization/binaryformatter-security-guide); [CA2300](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dotnet/fundamentals/code-analysis/quality-rules/ca2300); [CA2301](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dotnet/fundamentals/code-analysis/quality-rules/ca2301); [migration guide](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dotnet/standard/serialization/binaryformatter-migration-guide/)
- **Racket stateless servlets.** Serializable continuations are sent to the client via "stuffers." The docs warn: "Without explicit provision, it is possible for users to modify the continuations they are sent." `HMAC-SHA1-stuffer` "allows the servlet to certify that stuffed data was truly generated by the servlet" (20-byte MAC prepended), but "does not encrypt the data in anyway." The **default stuffer does not sign**. A GitHub issue (#68) raises that SHA1/MD5 are outdated. The stuffers doc existed by PLT Scheme 4.1.5 (c. 2009). — [Racket Stateless Servlets](https://docs.racket-lang.org/web-server/stateless.html); [PLT 4.1.5 stuffers doc](http://download.plt-scheme.org/doc/4.1.5/web-server/stuffers_ss.html); [issue #68](https://github.com/racket/web-server/issues/68)
- **Mobile-agent state appraisal.** William M. Farmer, Joshua D. Guttman, Vipin Swarup, "Security for Mobile Agents: Authentication and State Appraisal," ESORICS '96, LNCS 1146, pp. 118–130, Springer, 1996, DOI 10.1007/3-540-61770-1_31. An agent's state "can change in unwanted ways as it traverses multiple hosts". State-appraisal functions check invariants on arriving agent state, "to protect a host from attack … maintain critical state invariants, and to control privileges." — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/3-540-61770-1_31); [DBLP](https://dblp.uni-trier.de/rec/conf/esorics/FarmerGS96.html)
- **Proof-carrying code for agents.** George C. Necula and Peter Lee, "Safe, Untrusted Agents Using Proof-Carrying Code," in G. Vigna (ed.), *Mobile Agents and Security*, LNCS 1419, Springer, 1998. The code carries a proof checked against a host safety policy (memory safety, resource bounds, etc.). — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/3-540-68671-1_5); [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.5555/648051.746192)
- Also in LNCS 1419: S. Berkovits, J. D. Guttman, V. Swarup, "Authentication for Mobile Agents." — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.5555/648051.746184); [PDF](https://web.cs.wpi.edu/~guttman/pubs/sema-lncs98.pdf)

### Inferences
- The X1r0z attack uses exactly the fields that contribution (4) validates: method identity, resume index (bci and yield-point bounds), and locals (slot counts/types). That makes a strong, current motivating case: an unvalidated resumable-frame format is an arbitrary-jump primitive. The whitepaper can cite it as an attack that its validation blocks, as long as the check allows only the script's own methods and only valid yield-point indices.
- There are two established alternatives to structural validation: (a) integrity, where the host MACs the state (Racket HMAC stuffer), which is sufficient when only the host writes state; and (b) type allow-lists at the serializer (SerializationBinder, Java serialization filters). Structural validation adds defense when the state was legitimately produced but may be stale (code version changed), corrupted, or partly attacker-influenced. The whitepaper should say why a MAC alone is not enough, e.g. code-version skew across simulators, or a trust boundary between hosts, as in Farmer et al.'s multi-host setting.
- Slot-type checks and exact slot counts closely resemble JVM/CLR bytecode verification (type-consistent frames at each instruction), and Java's StackMapTable is a direct analog of per-yield-point slot typing. The "novel" part is applying verifier-style frame typing to *deserialized resume state*, not the checks themselves.

### Gaps
- Java serialization filtering (JEP 290, JDK 9 / backported 2017) is an obvious type-allow-list precedent. Its primary page was not fetched this session and should be verified before citing.
- Seaside (Smalltalk) continuation security, and any published analysis of forging continuation state in JavaFlow, Kilim or Brakes, were not researched.
- I found no peer-reviewed paper that specifically proposes structural validation of deserialized *stack frames* (method/bci/slot type/depth). This absence is weak evidence of novelty, not proof.
- Stack-depth limits on deserialization (for example, recursion limits in System.Text.Json's MaxDepth) are well known in general but were not verified as sources here.
